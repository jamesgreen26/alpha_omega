# Alpha Omega: the best possible wrapping and projection

A plan for the ideal world shape and planet projection, ignoring implementation cost and backwards compatibility. It covers concepts, the tile and seam layout, and the projection math. It deliberately leaves out how wrapping behavior is implemented (canonicalization, chunk aliasing, packet normalization and so on).

All numbers in this document were computed numerically and cross-checked. The projection was verified in Java and Python.

## 1. Goals and constraints

**Goals**

1. The world loops when you walk in any direction.
2. The sky behaves as on a real planet: celestial objects stay fixed relative to the terrain.
3. There are no visible seams, walls or teleports.
4. Every place exists exactly once: a true sphere, not a doubled one.

**Constraints**

- The ground is a flat grid of square blocks. The only symmetries it allows are translations and rotations by multiples of 90°. Reflections are excluded because they reverse handedness (stairs, text, redstone).
- A flat surface has no curvature, and a sphere has 4π of it (Gauss–Bonnet). The curvature can't be removed, only concentrated into a few points. The design question is where those points go and how evenly the sky moves everywhere else.

## 2. Concept: the world is a flat orbifold

The world is the infinite plane divided by a group `Γ` of grid-preserving motions. A player's position is a point in the plane, and all of its images under `Γ` are the same place. For the result to be a closed, sphere-like surface, `Γ` must contain rotations. The points those rotations fix become **cone points**, where the flat ground closes up around less than 360°.

Under the grid constraint there are two candidate groups:

| Group | Generated by | Cone points | Cone angle | Sky near a cone point |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **p2** | two translations and a 180° rotation | 4 | 180° | turns 2× per loop; speed ∝ distance |
| p4 | a square lattice and a 90° rotation | 3 (two of 90°, one of 180°) | 90° or 180° | turns 4× per loop; speed ∝ distance³ |

Rotations by 120° and 60° (p3, p6) don't preserve a square grid.

Under p2 the world has three nested descriptions:

```
plane  ──(lattice translations)──▶  torus  ──(180° rotation)──▶  sphere
(each player's frame)              (2 copies of each place)      (1 copy: the world)
```

- **Plane:** the frame a player experiences, perfectly flat and continuous.
- **Torus:** the plane modulo the lattice. Every place on the planet appears twice, as a pair rotated 180° from each other.
- **Sphere:** the torus modulo the 180° rotation. This is the actual world; every place exists once.

## 3. Choosing the group and lattice

How evenly the sky moves is measured by the share of the world where the sky turns at less than half its average speed per block walked. Lower is better.

| Option | Area below 50% of mean speed | Below 25% | Max / mean |
|---|---|---|---|
| Current `MeridianLoopProjection` (east–west speed) | 33.3% | 16.1% | — |
| p2, 2:1 rectangular lattice | 29.9% | — | — |
| p4, square lattice | 26.9% | 16.2% | 2.49 |
| p2, square lattice | 9.5% | 2.3% | 1.56 |
| **p2, hexagonal lattice** | **8.8%** | **2.2%** | **1.33** |

A search over lattice shapes, with the sphere placement optimized for each, confirmed the hexagonal lattice is the most even.

**Decision: p2 on a hexagonal lattice.** Its four cone points map to the vertices of a regular tetrahedron on the sphere, as far apart as four points can be.

## 4. Tile and seam layout

### Lattice

Coordinates are Minecraft `x` (east) and `z` (south). For a size factor `k`:

```
L1 = (a, 0)        a = 3840·k
L2 = (a/2, b)      b = 3328·k
```

`b/a = 0.86667`, against `√3/2 = 0.86603`. The projection absorbs the 0.07% difference exactly with a scale factor on `z` (section 6), so the sky is unaffected.

### Identifications at the default size (k = 4: a = 15360, b = 13312)

All of these are the same place as `(x, z)`:

- `(x ± 15360, z)`: east–west lap
- `(x ± 7680, z ± 13312)`: the diagonal lattice vectors
- `(−x, −10496 − z)`: 180° rotation about the north-pole cone point; a player's yaw turns 180°

Combining these gives a 180° rotation about every cone point.

### The canonical tile: a zipped cylinder with staggered zippers

One copy of the world is a strip `15360` wide (east–west) and `6656` tall (north–south):

```
 x: −7680            −3840              0               3840            7680 ≡ −7680
     F ─────────────────────────────── N ─────────────────────────────── F     z = −5248   north fold row
     │                                                                   │
     │                                  ★ spawn (0, −44)                 │     east–west seam:
     │                                                                   │     x = ±7680 joined
     │                                                                   │     by translation
     ──────────────── W ─────────────────────────────── E ───────────────      z = 1408    south fold row
```

The tile has three seams:

| Seam | Where | Crossing it |
|---|---|---|
| East–west | `x = ±7680` | Translate by `15360`. Ordinary looping. |
| North fold row | `z = −5248` | Arrive on the same row at `x → −x`, rotated 180°. Fixed at `N` and `F`. |
| South fold row | `z = 1408` | Arrive on the same row at `x → 7680 − x`, rotated 180°. Fixed at `E` and `W`. |

The fold points on the two rows are offset by a quarter of the circumference. That stagger is what makes the lattice hexagonal; a square lattice would line the fold points up.

None of the seams are visible to players, since each player's frame is the continuous plane. They describe the layout only.

### Cone points

| Point | World position | Latitude | Longitude |
|---|---|---|---|
| `N` | `(0, −5248)` | 90° (the north pole) | — |
| `F` | `(±7680, −5248)` | −19.47° | 180° |
| `E` | `(3840, 1408)` | −19.47° | 60° E |
| `W` | `(−3840, 1408)` | −19.47° | 60° W |

Every cone point sits on a chunk corner and a multiple of 128 blocks, so the Nether can use the same layout at 1:8. No block or chunk is its own image.

### Sizes

The fold rows are rounded to multiples of 128, so spawn sits a few dozen blocks from `z = 0`, at the exact point on the equator facing north.

| k | a × b | North row | South row | Spawn | Equivalent circumference | Mean sky speed | Sky speed at spawn | Speed below 50% within |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| small | 3584 × 3072 | −1152 | 384 | (0, 49) | ~4,160 | 8.3° / 100 | 10.7° / 100 | ~280 blocks |
| 2 | 7680 × 6656 | −2560 | 768 | (0, 42) | ~8,960 | 3.9° / 100 blocks | 5.0° / 100 | ~600 blocks of a cone point |
| **4** | **15360 × 13312** | **−5248** | **1408** | **(0, −44)** | **~17,900** | **1.9° / 100** | **2.5° / 100** | **~1,200 blocks** |
| 8 | 30720 × 26624 | −10368 | 2944 | (0, 40) | ~35,800 | 1.0° / 100 | 1.25° / 100 | ~2,400 blocks |

The small size is not a multiple of the base lattice: `b/a = 0.857`, 1% off `√3/2`, which the `z` scale factor `s` absorbs. Its fold row and spawn come from the same rule as the others: spawn is `0.3909·b` south of `N`, with the row rounded to 128. At this size the coarsest (4096-block) climate octaves contribute nothing.

Cone point x positions are always `0` and `±a/2` on the north row, and `±a/4` on the south row. "Equivalent circumference" is the circumference of a sphere with the world's area, `a·b/2`.

**The Nether** uses the same layout scaled by 1/8. At k = 4 that is a 1920 × 1664 lattice, with fold rows at `z = −656` and `176` and cone points at `x = 0`, `±960` and `±480`, all chunk-aligned.

If Nether alignment isn't needed, rounding the fold rows to multiples of 16 instead puts spawn within 4 blocks of `z = 0`.

## 5. Planet orientation

The sphere can be rotated freely onto the cone points. Requiring spawn at 0° 0° with world north pointing geographic north (so the area around spawn looks vanilla) leaves a choice of spawn location. Scanning every candidate gave this choice:

- **One cone point at the north pole (`N`), the other three at 19.47° S,** 120° apart in longitude.
- **Spawn is 61.9° from the nearest cone point.** The theoretical maximum for any point is 70.5°.
- **The north–south line through spawn is an exact mirror line of the map.** Walking due north from spawn follows the prime meridian straight to the north pole, `5204` blocks away at k = 4. The pole is a 180° cone point, so a straight line through it folds back on itself: continuing past it, you come straight back down the prime meridian, facing south, and reach spawn again after another `5204` blocks. Walking due south crosses the south pole.
- **Three of the special places coincide.** The north pole is already special for the sun and local time; putting a cone point there means one fewer special place.

Landmarks at k = 4:

| Walk from spawn | Arrive at |
|---|---|
| 2,600 blocks north | 63.1° N, on the prime meridian |
| 5,204 blocks north | The north pole (cone point `N`) |
| 10,408 blocks north | Spawn again (0° 0°), facing south |
| 5,000 blocks south | 57.2° S, on the 180° meridian (past the south pole) |
| 3,840 blocks east | 15.1° S, 67.6° E, with world north now 57.7° east of geographic north |

**The alternative considered** was a spin axis through two opposite tetrahedron edges. It puts the cone points at ±35.26°, symmetric between hemispheres, keeps the whole equator at least 35° from every cone point, and leaves both poles ordinary. Requiring world north to point geographic north at spawn, however, forces spawn to within about 35° of a cone point. The chosen orientation favors a spawn far from every cone point and a clean meridian through it.

**Compass drift is inherent.** Straight lines on flat ground are not great circles on the sphere, so on a long straight walk the sky's directions rotate relative to world axes. The meridian through spawn is the one straight line that stays exact. On the square lattice, the east–west line through spawn would also be the equator, but at the cost of less even sky speed.

## 6. Projection math

### Inputs

```
s  = (√3/2) · a / b                        ≈ 0.99926 (makes the lattice exactly hexagonal)
(X₀, Z₀) = cone point N                    = (0, −5248) at k = 4
ζ  = ( (x − X₀) − i·s·(z − Z₀) ) / a       real = east, imaginary = north
τ  = e^{iπ/3}                              ζ lives on the lattice Z + τZ
```

### Weierstrass ℘ and its derivative

Reduce `ζ` near the origin first: subtract `round(Im ζ / Im τ)·τ`, then `round(Re ζ)`. With `N = 6` the series below reach double precision.

```
℘(ζ)  = π² [ Σ_{n=−N..N} csc²(π(ζ + nτ)) − Σ_{n≠0} csc²(πnτ) − 1/3 ]
℘′(ζ) = −2π³ Σ_{n=−N..N} csc²(π(ζ + nτ)) · cot(π(ζ + nτ))
e₁    = ℘(1/2) ≈ 5.898344      K = √2 · e₁ ≈ 8.341518
```

The hexagonal lattice is the equianharmonic case (`g₂ = 0`). Scaling by `K` places the finite branch values at the tetrahedral latitude.

### Latitude, longitude and heading

```
W        = −℘(ζ) / K
latitude = 2·atan|W| − π/2
longitude = −arg W                                     (east positive)
heading  = arg ℘(ζ) − arg ℘′(ζ) − π/2   (mod 2π)        (compass bearing of world −Z)
```

At `ζ ≡ 0` (cone point `N`), latitude is 90°, and longitude and heading are undefined. These closed forms are orientation-preserving: no reflection is needed or allowed.

### Sky speed

The rotation of the sky per block walked, in radians, is the same in every direction:

```
λ(x, z) = 2 |℘′(ζ)| / ( K · (1 + |W|²) ) / a
```

It is 0 exactly at the four cone points and grows linearly with distance from each.

### Verified properties

- Spawn maps to 0° 0° with heading 0.
- The cone points map to a regular tetrahedron: all six angles are 109.47°.
- Every identification in section 4 gives the same latitude and longitude. Under the 180° rotation the heading changes by exactly 180°.
- The map preserves angles and handedness everywhere except at the four cone points.

### Reference implementation (Java, projection only)

```java
/** Projection for the hexagonal p2 orbifold world (k = 4). */
public final class HexPlanet {
    static final double A = 15360, B = 13312;
    static final double X0 = 0, Z0 = -5248;                     // cone point N (north pole)
    static final double S = Math.sqrt(3) / 2 * A / B;
    static final double TR = 0.5, TI = Math.sqrt(3) / 2, PI = Math.PI;
    static final double E1 = wp(0.5, 0)[0];                      // ≈ 5.898344
    static final double K = Math.sqrt(2) * E1;

    /** {latitude, longitude, heading} in radians. */
    static double[] project(double x, double z) {
        double zr = (x - X0) / A, zi = -S * (z - Z0) / A;
        double[] p = wp(zr, zi);
        if (p == null) return new double[] {PI / 2, 0, 0};
        double wr = -p[0] / K, wi = -p[1] / K;
        double lat = 2 * Math.atan(Math.hypot(wr, wi)) - PI / 2;
        double lon = -Math.atan2(wi, wr);
        double[] d = wpPrime(zr, zi);
        double heading = Math.atan2(p[1], p[0]) - Math.atan2(d[1], d[0]) - PI / 2;
        heading -= 2 * PI * Math.floor(heading / (2 * PI));
        return new double[] {lat, lon, heading};
    }

    /** Sky rotation per block walked, in radians. */
    static double skySpeed(double x, double z) {
        double zr = (x - X0) / A, zi = -S * (z - Z0) / A;
        double[] p = wp(zr, zi), d = wpPrime(zr, zi);
        if (p == null) return 0;
        double w2 = (p[0] * p[0] + p[1] * p[1]) / (K * K);
        return 2 * Math.hypot(d[0], d[1]) / K / (1 + w2) / A;
    }

    static double[] reduce(double re, double im) {
        long n = Math.round(im / TI); re -= n * TR; im -= n * TI; re -= Math.round(re);
        return new double[] {re, im};
    }

    static double[] wp(double re, double im) {
        double[] r = reduce(re, im); re = r[0]; im = r[1];
        if (re * re + im * im < 1e-24) return null;
        double sr = 0, si = 0;
        for (int n = -6; n <= 6; n++) {
            double[] c = csc2(PI * (re + n * TR), PI * (im + n * TI));
            sr += c[0]; si += c[1];
            if (n != 0) { double[] k = csc2(PI * n * TR, PI * n * TI); sr -= k[0]; si -= k[1]; }
        }
        return new double[] {PI * PI * (sr - 1.0 / 3), PI * PI * si};
    }

    static double[] wpPrime(double re, double im) {
        double[] r = reduce(re, im); re = r[0]; im = r[1];
        double sr = 0, si = 0;
        for (int n = -6; n <= 6; n++) {
            double a = PI * (re + n * TR), b = PI * (im + n * TI);
            double[] c = csc2(a, b), t = cot(a, b);
            sr += c[0] * t[0] - c[1] * t[1]; si += c[0] * t[1] + c[1] * t[0];
        }
        double f = -2 * PI * PI * PI;
        return new double[] {f * sr, f * si};
    }

    static double[] sin(double a, double b) { return new double[] {Math.sin(a) * Math.cosh(b), Math.cos(a) * Math.sinh(b)}; }
    static double[] cos(double a, double b) { return new double[] {Math.cos(a) * Math.cosh(b), -Math.sin(a) * Math.sinh(b)}; }

    static double[] csc2(double a, double b) {
        double[] s = sin(a, b); double r = s[0] * s[0] - s[1] * s[1], i = 2 * s[0] * s[1], d = r * r + i * i;
        return new double[] {r / d, -i / d};
    }

    static double[] cot(double a, double b) {
        double[] s = sin(a, b), c = cos(a, b); double d = s[0] * s[0] + s[1] * s[1];
        return new double[] {(c[0] * s[0] + c[1] * s[1]) / d, (c[1] * s[0] - c[0] * s[1]) / d};
    }
}
```

The output is the same latitude, longitude and heading triple that `LocalSky` already consumes, so local time, sun direction and sky darkening carry over unchanged.

## 7. What players experience

- **No seams anywhere.** Walking in any direction loops back, and the ground is perfectly flat everywhere.
- **A planet-consistent sky.** Celestial objects stay fixed relative to the terrain. At k = 4, the sky turns about 1.9° per 100 blocks on average, and 2.5° at spawn.
- **Near a cone point, the sky stalls.** Its speed falls in proportion to distance, below half the mean within about 1,200 blocks at k = 4. Walking once around a cone point turns the sky twice.
- **Near a cone point, some blocks appear twice.** Within render distance of a cone point, a block and its 180° rotated image can both be in view. They are the same blocks: break one and the other breaks too. This is the only visible trace of the curvature on the ground.
- **The north pole is a cone point.** The sun circles the horizon as at any pole, and the local clock winds through two days of time zones around it instead of one.
- **Each place exists once.** There are no duplicate locations and no ambiguity in star navigation.

## 8. Terrain requirement

For seams to be invisible, every terrain function `f` has to be invariant under `Γ`. That means it must be:

- **periodic** on the hexagonal lattice: `f(p + L1) = f(p + L2) = f(p)`, and
- **even about cone point `N`**: `f(2N − p) = f(p)`.

Periodicity and evenness about `N` together imply evenness about all four cone points.

Two constructions satisfy both, with no other change to terrain character:

- **Lattice-hashed gradient noise.** Index the gradients by lattice cell, reduced modulo `L1` and `L2`, and give the cell at `2N − q` the 180°-rotated gradient of `q`: `(−gx, gy, −gz)`. Vanilla's gradient set is closed under that rotation. This works for octaves whose cell size divides `a/2`, `b` and both coordinates of `2N`; at k = 4, that includes every octave up to 256-block cells.
- **Spectral noise** for the coarsest octaves: a sum of `cos(2π k·(p − N))` over vectors `k` of the dual lattice, with random amplitudes. Cosines are even and lattice-periodic by construction.

The only statistical side effect: at each cone point, `f` is forced to have zero gradient and its variance is locally higher.

## 9. Alternatives considered

| Alternative | Verdict |
|---|---|
| Square torus with `MeridianLoopProjection` (current) | Two whole rows collapse to the poles, east–west speed is uneven, and the far side is mirrored |
| p2 square double cover (`WeierstrassProjection`) | Very good. East–west through spawn is the equator. Somewhat less even, and every place exists twice unless folded |
| **p2 hexagonal orbifold** | **Chosen: most even sky, each place once, tetrahedral cone points** |
| p4 square orbifold | Fewer cone points but much harsher: 27% of the world is slow |
| Rectangular (2:1) lattice | Cone points pair up near the poles; Mercator-like distortion (30% slow) |
| Cube or pillowcase worlds | Visible geometric defects in the ground |
| Roaming cone points | Cone points can only move through unedited terrain; adds complexity without making the sky more even |
